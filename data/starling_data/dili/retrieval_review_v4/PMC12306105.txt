Abstract
Background
Ischemic heart disease remains a leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide, with myocardial ischemia–reperfusion (I/R) injury significantly contributing to cardiomyocyte death and poor outcomes post-acute myocardial infarction (AMI). Emerging evidence highlights metabolic changes during myocardial injury, particularly in purine metabolism. This study investigates the protective role of xanthosine (XTS), a purine metabolism intermediate, in alleviating I/R injury.
Methods
Neonatal and adult mouse myocardial tissues post-myocardial infarction (MI) were analyzed using untargeted and targeted metabolomics to explore metabolic profiles. The effects of XTS on I/R injury were evaluated in vivo using a murine I/R model and in vitro with hypoxia/reoxygenation-treated neonatal rat cardiomyocytes (NRCMs). Cardiac function, fibrosis, apoptosis, oxidative stress markers, and ferroptosis-related pathways were assessed via echocardiography, biochemical assays, western blotting, and electron microscopy. Integrated drug affinity responsive target stability (DARTS)-based drug target screening and RNA-seq transcriptomic profiling elucidate XTS-mediated mechanisms against I/R injury. 
Results
Metabolomics revealed distinct differences in purine metabolism between neonatal and adult mice post-MI, with significant XTS accumulation observed in neonatal hearts. In vivo, XTS treatment in adult mice enhanced left ventricular function, reduced fibrosis, and alleviated lipid peroxidation and mitochondrial damage post-I/R injury. In vitro, XTS significantly improved cardiomyocyte viability, reduced oxidative stress, and mitigated ferroptosis by restoring glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) levels and reducing acyl-coenzyme A synthetase long-chain family member 4 (ACSL4) expression. Mechanistically, XTS stabilized metabolic enzymes, upregulated l-arginine and glutathione (GSH) to mitigate reactive oxygen species(ROS), and inhibited ferroptosis.
Conclusions
XTS, a key purine metabolism intermediate, improves cardiac remodeling and function following I/R injury by suppressing ferroptosis and reducing mitochondrial ROS production. These findings provide novel insights into the therapeutic potential of XTS as an adjunctive treatment for patients with AMI undergoing revascularization.

Introduction
Ischemic heart disease, especially acute myocardial infarction (AMI), still remains one of the leading causes of morbidity and mortality worldwide [1–3]. In the infarcted myocardium, ischemia-induced elevation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) can significantly promote cardiomyocyte death and adverse fibrosis [4, 5]. Despite the significant benefits of timely coronary reperfusion therapy in alleviating ischemic myocardium, these patient populations are still at high risk of mortality owing to secondary ischemia–reperfusion (I/R) injury [6]. Investigating the underlying mechanisms of myocardial I/R injury can provide critical insights for developing the specific therapeutic strategy to reduce myocardial damage. Simultaneously, improvement of the strict regulation of cell death and injury responses may also be crucial for tissue repair, functional recovery, and the maintenance of cardiac health [7]. Therefore, in-depth investigation into the mechanisms of myocardial I/R injury, especially programmed cardiomyocyte death, will be vital for enhancing the prognosis of these patient subsets [8].
Postnatally, mammals are exposed to an oxygen-rich environment [5, 9], inducing significant metabolic reprogramming in cardiomyocytes and shifting their metabolic profile from anaerobic glycolysis to oxidative phosphorylation [10, 11]. This shift not only enables efficient adenosine triphosphate (ATP) synthesis but also results in increased mitochondrial ROS production. Simultaneously, the purine nucleotide degradation pathway (xanthine metabolic pathway) will be upregulated in mammals after birth [12] and serves as a major source of cytosolic ROS under conditions of ischemia–reperfusion and inflammation [13]. Overall, neonatal hearts may reduce mitochondrial and cytosolic ROS production by maintaining glycolysis and minimizing nucleotide catabolism. Of note, the heart is the most metabolically active organ, and the metabolic processes can influence various physiological pathways, including myocardial injury, repair, and regeneration [14, 15]. The rapid development of metabolomics has enabled a comprehensive analysis of the molecular changes in various metabolites under physiological or pathological conditions [16]. Previous studies have identified various metabolites as critical regulators of cardiac function and key contributors to myocardial repair and regeneration after AMI [17–19]. Recent studies have also shown that neonatal hearts can exhibit much lower and shorter-lasting levels of lipid peroxidation after MI when compared with that in adult hearts, suggesting significantly enhanced antioxidant defenses and recovery potential in neonatal hearts [20]. Therefore, these differences in metabolic patterns and relevant metabolites between neonatal and adult mouse hearts after MI warrant further investigation urgently, which may provide critical insights into novel strategies for myocardial injury repair. In the present study, we compared the cardiac metabolome of neonatal and adult mice following AMI, revealing significant fluctuations in the purine nucleotide metabolism pathway. Notably, the purine metabolic intermediate xanthosine (XTS) was found to be gradually degraded during development, with a remarkable accumulation after AMI in neonatal hearts. Accordingly, further in-depth explorations for the efficacy of XTS on I/R injury were performed. 
Materials and methods
Establishment of I/R models and drug administration
All animal-related protocols in the present study were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Southeast University (Nanjing, China, 20240219008) and conducted in accordance with the National Institutes of Health (NIH) guidelines. C57BL/6 or ICR mice (8 weeks old, male) were used for establishing the I/R models. These mice were housed in pathogen-free conditions with constant temperature of 20 ± 2 °C, humidity of 50–70%, and a regular 12 h light–dark cycle. Besides, standard chow and water were timely applied for these animals.
After 1 week of acclimatization, these mice received intraperitoneal anesthesia with sodium pentobarbital and then randomly underwent the indexed I/R surgery as previously described (n = 12) [21] or sham operations (n = 12). Briefly, a 7–0 surgical suture was used for ligating the left anterior descending (LAD) coronary artery with a slipknot, one thread end of which was exposed outside the thoracic cavity. One hour later, the slipknot was released to allow reperfusion of the heart. Conversely, sham-operated mice underwent all the surgical procedures but without ligation of the proximal LAD coronary artery. Notably, these mice were maintained on a thermal plate with a constant temperature of 37 °C during the whole procedure.
To better evaluate the effectiveness and safety of XTS, we dissolved this compound (100 mg/kg, HY-W011527, Med Chem Express, China) in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, HY-Y0320, Med Chem Express, China), which was then diluted with sterile saline to ensure a final DMSO concentration of 5%. The prepared solution was administered to the mice via intraperitoneal injection 1 h prior to reperfusion, and then continued once daily (labeled Sham + XTS or I/R + XTS). For the control groups, an equal volume of saline solution containing 5% DMSO alone was administered similarly to the residual mice (denoted as Sham + DMSO or I/R + DMSO). Additionally, to provide a more comprehensive understanding of its effects, our animal experiments were conducted with these interventions at five different time points concurrently.
Echocardiography
Transthoracic M-mode and two-dimensional echocardiography was performed to assess the cardiac functions and left ventricular (LV) structures of these mice at 1 day, 7 days, and 14 days after surgery, respectively, using the Vevo 2100 Imaging System (FUJIFILM VisualSonics, Toronto, Canada) with a 30-MHz phased array linear transducer. All mice were anesthetized by inhalation of 3% isoflurane initially and maintained with 1.5% isoflurane during examination. The examined items mainly included the left ventricular internal diameter at end-diastole (LVIDd), internal diameter at systole (LVIDs), end-diastolic volume (EDV), and end-systolic volume (ESV). The LV ejection fraction (EF%) and fractional shortening (FS%) were then calculated as described previously [22].
2,3,5-Triphenyltetrazolium chloride (TTC)–Evans blue double staining
TTC–Evans blue staining was used for identifying the ischemic myocardium and calculating the infarct size. Twenty-four hours after surgery, the mice were euthanized and injected with 1.5% Evans blue dye (approximately 0.2 mL for each 25 g mouse) through the thoracic aorta after the occlusion of the LAD artery. These hearts were then harvested rapidly and rinsed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to remove any superficial dye. Of note, the tissues were frozen at −20 °C for 30 min, followed by uniform slicing from the ligation point to the apex, with thickness of 1 mm. Subsequently, these tissue sections were incubated with 1% TTC solution for staining, then observed under a stereomicroscope for image acquisition. The results were quantitatively analyzed by using ImageJ software. 
Histology and immunofluorescence staining
The infarcted heart tissues were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and then were sectioned into 3-μm-thick slices after embedding with paraffin. These slices underwent the following staining:
For cardiomyocyte proliferation measurements, the sections were incubated with the following primary antibodies: cTnT (2 ug/ml, ab8295, Abcam, UK) and Ki67 (1:100, ab15580, Abcam, UK) after blocking with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) containing 0.2% Triton X-100. Thereafter, these sections were then incubated with species-matched Alexa Fluor 488 or Alexa Fluor 594 secondary antibodies (1:500, Biosharp, China) and counterstained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI; G1012-10ML, Servicebio Technology, China). Finally, the stained tissue sections were subsequently observed using Case Viewer (Wuhan Servicebio Technology Co., LTD). 
Wheat germ agglutinin (WGA; G1730-100UL, Servicebio Technology, China) and DAPI (G1012-10ML, Servicebio Technology, China) staining was used to determine cross-sectional area (CSA). The stained tissue sections were subsequently observed using Case Viewer (Wuhan Servicebio Technology Co., LTD). Cardiomyocyte CSA was analyzed by using ImageJ software (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA; http://rsb.info.nih.gov/nih-image/).
Cardiomyocyte apoptosis was evaluated using a terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick-end labeling (TUNEL) staining kit (G1505-50 T, Servicebio Technology, Wuhan, China) following the manufacturer’s guidelines. The stained tissue sections were subsequently observed using Case Viewer (Wuhan Servicebio Technology Co., LTD). 
Untargeted metabolomics analysis
Sample preparation
Cardiac tissue samples were thawed on ice. A 30-mg portion of the tissue was then homogenized with 400 μL of extraction solution (methanol:acetonitrile = 3:1, precooled to −40 °C). The mixture was vortexed for 5 min, subjected to ultrasonic treatment in an ice–water bath for 10 min, and incubated at 4 °C for 2 h. The supernatant after centrifugation (4 °C, 12,000 rpm for 15 min) was carefully collected and then vacuum concentrated to dryness. Moreover, the residue was reconstituted with 100 μL of 50% methanol aqueous solution (methanol:water = 1:1, v/v), vortexed for 3 min at 4 °C and 2000 rpm, and centrifuged again at 12,000 rpm and 4 °C for 15 min. The resulting supernatant was collected for mass spectrometry analysis.
LC–MS/MS analysis
Sample separation was carried out using an ultra-high performance liquid chromatography (UHPLC) system equipped with an HSS T3 chromatographic column at 40 °C (injection volume: 2 μL, flow rate: 0.3 mL/min). Each sample was analyzed in both positive and negative ion modes using electrospray ionization (ESI). After separation, the samples were analyzed by a Thermo QE HF-X mass spectrometer.
Data processing
Peak identification and extraction were performed using Compound Discoverer version 3.3. Missing values were imputed using linear regression, and the data were normalized based on the maximum median peak area across all samples. Statistical significance testing and classification analyses were conducted using R and Python programming languages. Differential metabolites were identified based on a fold change (FC) threshold of ≥ 2 and a P-value <0.05. The identified metabolites, categorized as upregulated or downregulated, were further analyzed through principal component analysis (PCA) and clustering to ensure a comprehensive understanding of the metabolic profiles.
Targeted metabolomics analysis
Sample preparation
Cardiac tissue samples were prepared similarly as above-mentioned.
LC–MS analysis
Multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) analysis was performed using a Thermo Vanquish UHPLC system coupled with a TSQ Altis high-resolution mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Data acquisition was controlled by XCalibur 4.4 software (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and data processing was performed using TraceFinder 5.1 software (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Ion channel information is provided in the Supplementary Table.
Isolation of neonatal rat cardiomyocytes (NRCMs), establishment of H/R model, and treatments
NRCMs were isolated and cultured as previously described [15]. Briefly, heart tissues from new-born Sprague–Dawley rats (1–2-day-old) were cut into fragments of 1 mm3 and incubated with Hank’s balanced salt solution (HBSS) containing 0.1% trypsin and 0.05% collagenase type II for further digestion. After filtration and 2 h of differential adhesion, the isolated NRCMs were cultivated in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM) enriched with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) for 48 h and replaced with a serum-free medium for 12 h before treatments. For establishing the hypoxia/reoxygenation (H/R) model, these NRCMs were first incubated in FBS-free and glucose-free 1640 medium (#11966-025, Gibco, USA) under hypoxic conditions (94% nitrogen, 5% carbon dioxide, and 1% oxygen). After a 12-h hypoxic period, the NRCMs were transferred to DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and then cultured in an incubator with 5% CO2 and 95% nitrogen for reoxygenation, which was maintained for 24 h. Before exposure to hypoxia and reoxygenation, the metabolites identified by the above-mentioned metabolomics analysis (XTS, 10 μM) were provided to these NRCMs in either the normoxia or H/R group for further exploration. Of note, XTS should be applied dissolved in DMSO. Besides, the parallel control group received an equal volume of empty DMSO to avoid confounders. 
The H9c2 cell line, procured from the Shanghai Institute of Cell Biology at the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China), was cultivated under analogous conditions to those used for NRCMs. Upon achieving 70% confluence, the culture medium was swapped for a serum-free version, which was maintained for 12 h. Subsequently, the cells were subjected to a 6-h period of hypoxia, followed by a 4-h phase of reoxygenation. Prior to this hypoxic/reoxygenation process, varying concentrations of XTS or DMSO were administered as previously described.
Cell viability assay
Cell viability was assessed using the CCK-8 assay kit (BS350A, Biosharp, China) and Trypan Blue staining kit (ST798, Beyotime, China) according to the manufacturers’ instructions. After incubating for 1–4 h, the absorbance at 450 nm was measured for assessing the cell viability. Besides, these transfected NRCMs and H9c2 cells (approximately 1 × 105) were stained using Trypan Blue, then counted using a microscope (Nikon, Japan). Afterwards, the cell viability was determined according to the formula (number of viable cells/total number of cells) × 100%, providing a clear and direct measure of cell health and survival rate.
Measurement of drug affinity responsive target stability (DARTS)-LC/MS
DARTS-LC/MS was used to pinpoint potential endogenous targets of XTS in H9c2 cells as previously described [23]. Peptide separation and analysis were performed using an Easy-nLC 1200 system coupled to an Orbitrap Fusion mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Raw MS data were processed with MaxQuant 1.5.4.1 (MS tolerance: 5 ppm; MS/MS tolerance: 20 ppm) and searched against the UniProt Database (2016_07 release, 70,630 entries) along with the MaxQuant Proteomics Contaminant Database for protein identification.
RNA-seq analysis
Following treatment with DMSO or XTS (10 μM), these H9c2 cells (1 × 106) which had undergone H/R injury were preserved in liquid nitrogen for subsequent RNA-Seq analysis, conducted by Beijing Novogene Biotechnology Co., Ltd. and Shanghai NewCore Biotechnology Co., Ltd., as previously described [24].
Cellular thermal shift assay (CETSA)
H9c2 cells were seeded in two 10-cm2 culture dishes. One dish was treated with 10 μM XTS, while the other received an equivalent volume of DMSO as a control. After incubation, cells were harvested and resuspended in PBS. The resuspended cell aliquots were divided into six microtubes and subjected to thermal challenge at seven temperature gradients ranging from 40 to 55 °C for 3 min. Subsequently, the samples were flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen. Post-heating, three freeze–thaw cycles were performed using liquid nitrogen to ensure complete cell lysis. The lysates were centrifuged at 12,000×g for 20 min at 4 °C to collect the supernatant, and equal volumes of proteins were analyzed by Western blotting using target-specific antibodies.
Detection of oxidative stress markers
The concentrations of relevant biomarkers with respect to oxidative stress in the NRCMs were measured using the corresponding commercial kit, including malondialdehyde (MDA, # A003-4-1), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH, # A020-2-2), and superoxide dismutase (SOD, # A001-3-2), which were all purchased from Nanjing Jiancheng Biotechnology Research Institute (China) and performed following the manufacturer’s instructions.
ATP detection
ATP levels were measured using an ATP Bioluminescence Kit (A22066, Invitrogen, USA,) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Heart tissues or the NRCMs were lysed first and added to a 96-well plate, respectively. The absorbance at 560 nm was measured, then ATP levels were calculated on the basis of a standard curve.
Measurement of ROS and mitochondrial ROS (mROS)
ROS and mROS levels were measured using the H2DCFDA probe (HY-D0940, MedChemExpress, China), MitoSOX™ Red mitochondrial superoxide indicator (HY-D1055, Med Chem Express, China) and dihydroethidium (DHE, HY-D0079, Med Chem Express, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, respectively. All images were acquired using an inverted fluorescence microscope (BZ-X810, KEYENCE, Japan) or a confocal microscope (FV3000, Olympus, Japan) and analyzed with ImageJ software.
Detection of mitochondrial membrane potential (Δψm)
The mitochondrial membrane potential (Δψm) of these NRCMs was assessed using the JC-1 fluorescent probe (C2005, Beyotime, China), adhering to the manufacturer’s guidelines. Under a fluorescence microscope, the cells were observed with the excitation wavelength set at 488 nm to reveal strong green fluorescence and at 594 nm to reveal striking red fluorescence.
Measurement of the mitochondrial oxygen consumption rate (OCR)
The mitochondrial oxygen consumption (OCR) of NRCMs was evaluated using a MitoStress Test kit (cat. no. 103015-100; Agilent) (1 × 104 cells/well) according to the manufacturer’s instructions and protocols. The calibration plate and probe plate were then carefully inserted into an Agilent Seahorse Bioscience XF96 extracellular flux analyzer, where the OCR was precisely calculated using the Wave software provided by Agilent.
Biochemical analyses
The blood samples were centrifuged (5000 rpm) for 15 min at 4 °C to collect the serum for further biochemical measurements. According to the corresponding manufacturer’s instructions, the concentration levels of cTnI at 0.5, 3, and 6 days after surgery were consecutively detected using a standard enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay kit (#E-EL-M1203, Elabscience, China), as well as the LDH levels at 6 days after surgery (LDH Assay Kit, # MAK066, Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA). All measurements were performed in duplicate.
Magnetic resonance imaging protocol
For further assessing the alterations of cardiac structures and functions after I/R injury, cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (cMRI) was performed at 3 days after surgery. A dedicated small-animal MRI system (BioSpec70/20, Bruker, Germany) equipped with AVANCE III electronics and Paravision 6.0 software was used as previously described [25]. All mice were anesthetized via inhaling 3% isoflurane initially and maintained with 1.5% isoflurane during examinations. After standard cardiac localization, cine images were acquired and reconstructed using electrocardiogram-triggered fast low-angle shot sequences in four or five short-axis planes encompassing the entire left ventricle. All images were analyzed using dedicated software (QMass MR v.7.6, Medis, Leiden, The Netherlands, and ImageJ software). Edema size was quantified from the extent of hyperintense areas on T2-weighted images and calculated as a proportion of the total left ventricular area.
Real-time fluorescence quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) analysis
To measure the expression levels of the associated genes, heart samples were analyzed via RT-qPCR. Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (R411-01, Vazyme, China), then transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) using the HiScript II Q Select SuperMix (R233-01, Vazyme, China). The amplification of cDNA was subsequently performed following the protocol (95 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles at 95 °C for 5 s, 60 °C for 20 s) of the SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Q711-02, Vazyme, China) using the iCycler iQ system (Bio-Rad). The expressions of purine nucleoside phosphorylase (PNP) and cytosolic purine 5′-nucleotidase (NT5C2) were measured, and 18S was chosen as an internal control. The primer sequences of relative genes were as follows: 18S: 5′-GGAGTTCCTTGCGATGGTGA-3′, 3′-GGCCAGAAGCTCTGCTACTC-5′; PNP: 5′-GTGTGTGCGACACCGTACT-3′, 3′-CTCGGCTAGGTAGCGGTAG-5′; NT5C2: 5′-TCAAAGAAACGGCAAGGGTGGAG-3′, 3′-GCTGTCCAGGTGCTTGTAGAGTTC-5′. All samples were analyzed in duplicate, and the comparative Cq method (2-ΔΔCq) was used for quantification. 
Western blot analysis
Protein homogenates were extracted from heart tissues and NRCMs using radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) lysis buffer (C1053, Applygen, China). A bicinchoninic acid (BCA) protein assay kit (BL521A, Biosharp, China) was used to detect their concentrations. Thereafter, protein samples (20–40 μg) were separated on 8–12% sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS)-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE) as appropriate and transferred to a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane. After blocking in 5% skim milk for 1–2 h at 37 °C, these membranes were incubated with the specific primary antibodies. Detection was performed using enhanced chemiluminescence system (BL521A, Biosharp, China), and the intensity of the protein bands was analyzed using ImageJ software. The primary antibodies in this study were as followed: GPX4 (#ab125066, Abcam), ACSL4 (#ab155282, Abcam), NT5C2 (#15223-1-AP, Proteintech), PNP(#A19885, Abclonal), BAX (#50599-2-Ig, Proteintech), PHGDH (#YP-mAb-12503, UpingBio), GAA (#YP-mAb-12503, UpingBio), XPP1(#YP-mAb-06825, UpingBio), ALDH1A(#YP-mAb-11363, UpingBio), EPHB3 (#YP-mAb-12915, UpingBio), and UMPS (#14830-1-AP, Proteintech). The expression of GAPDH (#60,004–1-Ig, Proteintech) or HSP90 (#13171-1-AP, Proteintech) was chosen as an internal control as appropriate. 
Evaluation of mitochondrial morphology
The electron microscopy was used for assessing the mitochondrial morphology in these NRCMs. After fixing with 2.5% glutaraldehyde at room temperature for 5 min, these cells were collected (a cell scraper was used to scrap these cells at a single direction to avoid repeated damage) and then centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 2 min. We gently resuspended the cells in an adequate volume of electron microscope fixative, then sectioned the fixed cells and examined them under the electron microscope. Mitochondrial damage was assessed utilizing the Flameng scoring system.
Measurement of intracellular Fe2+
Intracellular Fe2⁺ levels were detected using the FerroOrange fluorescent probe (F374, Dojindo, Kumamoto, Japan) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, the NRCMs were cultured in conditioned medium in confocal dishes. Next, the cells were rinsed and incubated with 250 µM FerroOrange for 30 min. Afterwards, the cells were observed under a confocal microscope (FV3000, Olympus, Japan) and analyzed using FV3000 and ImageJ software.
Lipid peroxide measurement
Lipid peroxides were measured using the Liperfluo fluorescent probe (L248, Dojindo, Kumamoto, Japan) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. An inverted fluorescence microscope was used for further observation, and the acquired images were analyzed using ImageJ software.
Statistical analysis
All results are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and GraphPad Prism software (version 8.0, GraphPad Software, Inc., CA, USA) was applied for all analyses. Comparisons between multiple groups were conducted using one-way or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). A two-tailed P-value <0.05 or Q-value <0.05 (false discovery rate (FDR)-adjusted) was considered statistically significant. 
Results
Metabolic profiling revealed significant metabolic differences between neonatal and adult mice post-myocardial infarction using nontargeted metabolomics
To explore the metabolic disparities in the myocardium of neonatal versus adult mice after AMI, we employed nontargeted metabolomics to scrutinize heart tissues from four distinct groups: neonatal mice on day 6 post-MI (P1M6), neonatal mice on day 6 post-sham surgery (P1S6), adult mice on day 6 post-MI (P56M6), and adult mice on day 6 post-sham surgery (P56S6). Data preprocessing and subsequent validation were conducted through targeted metabolomics (Fig. 1A). As shown in Fig. 1B, a total of 302 metabolites were identified spanning various categories, among which the amino acids and their derivatives (59 species), as well as the nucleotides (37 species) were the predominant categories. To validate the quality and stability of the data, the relative standard deviation (RSD) distribution of metabolites in quality control (QC) samples were further analyzed, indicating that 49.7% of the metabolites had an RSD of less than 10%, with only 15.2% exceeding 20%, suggesting a much high quality and reliability of the relevant analyses (Supplementary Fig. 1A). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway classification analysis revealed that these metabolites were primarily involved in amino acid metabolism (80 metabolites, 9.8%), lipid metabolism (85 metabolites, 10.4%), carbohydrate metabolism (40 metabolites, 4.9%), and nucleotide metabolism (30 metabolites, 3.7%) (Fig. 1C). PCA was conducted to assess the differences among sample groups and the consistency of the data and demonstrated clear clustering, with the P1M6, P1S6, P56M6, and P56S6 groups forming distinct clusters, indicating significant metabolic differences among the treatment groups (Fig. 1D).

Purine metabolism exhibited significant changes in neonatal and adult mice post-MI
The heatmap illustrated significant metabolic changes across different experimental groups (Fig. 2A), and neonatal mice exhibited distinct alterations in metabolites when compared with adult mice following MI (Fig. 2B–C). KEGG enrichment analysis revealed that differential metabolites in neonatal mice post-MI were primarily enriched in nucleotide and purine metabolism pathways (Fig. 2D). In contrast, differential metabolites in adult mice were predominantly associated with amino acid and cofactor biosynthesis, alongside notable changes in nucleotide and purine metabolism pathways (Fig. 2E). Additionally, enrichment analysis using the Small Molecule Pathway Database (SMPDB) further highlighted substantial fluctuations in the purine metabolism pathway after MI, observed consistently in both neonatal and adult mice (Supplementary Fig. 2A, B). However, recent studies reported remarkable fluctuations in the purine metabolism pathway among the hearts of neonatal versus adult mice [12]. To further explain this controversial phenomenon in the present study, we compared purine metabolism-associated metabolites in the hearts of neonatal and adult mice post-MI and mapped the identified metabolites onto the purine metabolic pathway (Supplementary Fig. 2C). As shown in Fig. 2F, three distinct modules representing different patterns of metabolite changes were markedly identified. Briefly, metabolites in module 3 were significantly elevated in hearts of adult mice, including the downstream metabolites of the purine degradation pathway, such as xanthine and uric acid, which were elevated in both neonatal and adult mice post-MI. Similarly, metabolites in module 2 were also elevated in hearts of adult mice but showed significant reductions in both neonatal and adult mice post-MI. These metabolites mainly included the products and intermediates of the purine synthesis pathway, such as ribose-5-phosphate, inosine-5′-monophosphate (IMP), adenosine monophosphate (AMP), and guanosine monophosphate (GMP). In contrast, metabolites in module 1 exhibited a decreasing trend in hearts of adult mice, and this module primarily consisted of purine metabolic intermediates without distinct characteristics. Neonatal mice exhibited superior cardiac repair capabilities after MI, prompting further investigation into metabolite changes associated with this phenomenon. In-depth analysis of the expression trends of metabolites in module 1 revealed that XTS was the only metabolite significantly upregulated in neonatal mice post-MI, whereas it remained unchanged in adult hearts under the same conditions. This observation suggested that the alteration of XTS may be potentially linked to the enhanced cardiac repair capacity in neonatal mice.

XTS was significantly accumulated in the infarcted myocardium of neonatal mice
To further elucidate the dynamic changes of XTS, as well as its neighboring purine metabolites after MI in neonatal mice, we performed targeted metabolomic analyses on the hearts of neonatal mice at 3 days (P1M3), 6 days (P1M6), and 9 days (P1M9) post-MI, along with age-matched sham-operated controls (Sham) (Fig. 3A). The results revealed that IMP levels in the neonatal mouse hearts were significantly reduced after MI, whereas uric acid levels were markedly elevated instead. Additionally, XTS levels showed a significant increase in the early phase of MI (day 3), accompanied by a slightly increased trend in its upstream metabolite XMP and downstream metabolite xanthine. XTS is a purine nucleoside involved in various enzymatic reactions. It is synthesized by NT5C2 and can be converted to xanthine by PNP [26]. To investigate the cause of XTS accumulation in neonatal myocardial tissue following MI, we measured the protein expression levels of NT5C2 and PNP at day 3 post-MI, indicating that, the NT5C2 levels remained constant while PNP levels were significantly decreased in neonatal mice post-MI (Fig. 3B). In contrast, the PNP levels were increased in adult mice post-MI (Fig. 3C). This differential expression of catabolic enzymes might be the leading cause of the accumulation of XTS in neonatal mice after MI.

Besides, targeted metabolomics further confirmed that XTS levels in myocardial tissue steadily decreased during neonatal development (Fig. 3D). We further validated that the NT5C2 messenger RNA (mRNA) and protein levels remained stable or gradually decreased during neonatal development while PNP mRNA and protein levels began to increase after postnatal day 7 (Fig. 3E, F). These findings suggested that the significant elevation of XTS levels in the early stages of MI in neonatal mice might be linked to their enhanced myocardial repair capacity. All taken together, we assumed the XTS as a key potential protective agent in cardiac remodeling after AMI, and further relevant confirmation in vivo and in vitro was performed, respectively. 
XTS treatment enhanced cardiac function and reduced fibrosis after ischemia–reperfusion injury in adult mice
Neonatal mouse hearts exhibited exceptional capacity for managing lipid peroxidation, and the purine degradation pathway was closely linked to ROS production and cardiomyocyte cell cycle exit. To delve into the function of XTS in adult hearts, we induced myocardial I/R injury through occluding the LAD coronary artery and subsequently restoring blood flow. We then administered XTS at different concentrations or DMSO as a control. The results revealed a notable correlation: as the concentration of XTS increased, LDH release decreased, achieving its most pronounced effect at a dosage of 100 mg/kg XTS (Supplementary Fig. 3A). Then, XTS was administered intraperitoneally with a dose of 100 mg/kg/day, starting 1 h prior to surgery and continuing daily (Fig. 4A). As depicted in Fig. 4B, XTS treatment significantly decreased the ratio of heart weight versus body weight at 21 days post I/R injury. Similarly, ELISA results showed that XTS treatment had no significant effect on serum cTnI peak levels at 3 days after I/R surgery but significantly promoted the reduction of serum cTnI levels at 6 days after I/R surgery (Fig. 4C). These results indicated the reduced damaged myocardium with respect to XTS treatment. Besides, XTS treatment significantly decreased the levels of lipid peroxidation products in the I/R damaged myocardium, including 4-hydroxynonenal (4-HNE) and malondialdehyde (MDA) (Fig. 4D, E). Additionally, echocardiography results showed that XTS treatment significantly improved left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) and fractional shortening (FS) in these mice (Fig. 4F, G). Besides, T2-weighted images from the cMRI demonstrated that XTS could alleviate edema induced by I/R injury (3-day) (Fig. 4H). The attenuation of myocardial fibrosis after I/R injury was also confirmed to be related with XTS treatment by the Masson’s staining results (Fig. 4I), and TTC staining further confirmed the benefits of XTS treatment in the reduction of infarct size (Fig. 4J). To explore whether XTS treatment could improve cardiac functions after I/R injury via promoting cardiomyocyte proliferation or hypertrophy, we continuously administered XTS intraperitoneally from day 7 to day 14 post-surgery and the Ki67 and WGA staining was performed for further evaluation (Supplementary Fig. 3B). As shown in Supplementary Fig. 3C, D, XTS treatment had no significant effects on cardiomyocyte proliferation or size. These results indicated that XTS treatment could provide significant cardiac protection from I/R injury in terms of reducing myocardial fibrosis and improving cardiac functions in adult mice.

Explorations for the underlying mechanisms in the improvement of cardiac functions after I/R injury with respect to XTS
Multiple cell death mechanisms, including apoptosis and ferroptosis, can play crucial roles in the early stage of I/R injury [27]. After 1 h of ischemia followed by 24 h of reperfusion, XTS treatment showed no significant effects on apoptosis in the I/R-damaged myocardium (Supplementary Fig. 4A, B). Conversely, XTS treatment was found to be closely linked with ferroptosis in these I/R damaged myocardium on the basis of the results of western blot analyses. As shown in Fig. 5A, the expression of glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) in the I/R group, a key inhibitor of ferroptosis, was notably diminished. However, XTS treatment could effectively restore its protein levels. Conversely, I/R injury led to markedly increased protein levels of ferroptosis-promoting molecules, such as acyl-coenzyme A synthetase long-chain family member 4 (ACSL4), but XTS treatment could successfully mitigate this upregulation.

For further identifying the regulating effects of XTS on ferroptosis, we conducted in vitro experiments in both H9c2 cell lines and NRCMs. First, H9c2 cell lines were subjected to H/R therapy followed by treatment with different concentrations of XTS. The cell viability after H/R was assessed and the results confirmed the efficacy of XTS treatment, with the most pronounced effect at a concentration of 10 μM (Supplementary Fig. 4C, D). Additionally, 10 μM of XTS treatment could significantly reversed the reduction of GPX4 protein levels in H9c2 cell lines under the H/R condition (Fig. 5B). Moreover, a significant enhancement of ATP production was also observed in these NRCMs receiving 10 μM of XTS treatment (Fig. 5C). Therefore, we chose the 10 μM of XTS as an effective treatment dosage in subsequent experiments. The NRCMs were isolated and subjected to H/R therapy, which then received the treated with 10 μM of XTS. In line with the results in vivo, the H/R-induced increase of LDH release in these NRCMs could be markedly mitigated by XTS treatment (Fig. 5D). Furthermore, XTS treatment could also significantly increase the GPX4 protein levels and reduce ACSL4 levels in NRCMs after H/R therapy (Fig. 5E). A significant alleviation of lipid peroxidation and iron deposition, along with a reduction in ROS and mROS levels, was observed in the NRCMs receiving XTS treatment (Fig. 5F–J). Moreover, markedly increased glutathione (GSH) levels was also found to be related with XTS treatment in the NRCMs after H/R therapy (Fig. 5K). Simultaneously, XTS treatment could significantly reduce the superoxide dismutase (SOD) and MDA levels (Fig. 5L, M). To delve into the potential of XTS in ameliorating mitochondrial dysfunction, we conducted a comprehensive series of functional assessments. Our JC-1 fluorescence analysis revealed a substantial surge in JC-1 monomers after I/R injury, indicating a pronounced decrease in mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm). Strikingly, concurrent treatment with XTS notably counteracted this detrimental shift (Fig. 5N). Moreover, real-time bioenergetic evaluations utilizing the Seahorse XF96 extracellular flux analyzer demonstrated that XTS administration significantly enhanced both basal and maximal OCR in cardiomyocytes subjected to H/R injury, as compared with untreated controls (Fig. 5O). Transmission electron microscopy revealed typical ferroptosis features in these NRCMs, including reduced mitochondrial volume, decreased cristae, outer membrane rupture, and lipid peroxidation accumulation, which would be restored by XTS treatment (Fig. 5P). These findings suggested that XTS might protect the cardiomyocytes from H/R injury by reducing oxidative stress and iron deposition through inhibiting the hypoxia-induced ferroptosis.
Screening and validation of potential XTS binding targets
To delve deeper into the molecular mechanics of XTS-induced ferroptosis inhibition, we utilized DARTS-LC-MS to pinpoint potential endogenous targets of XTS (Fig. 6A). Our Western blot analysis revealed a marked reduction in proteolytic degradation triggered by 0.1% Pronase following XTS treatment, especially evident among proteins within the 55–130 kDa bracket (Supplementary Fig. 5A). A volcano plot analysis of protein expression differences highlighted XTS’s role in preventing the breakdown of numerous proteins (Fig. 6B). By scrutinizing molecular weights and annotating functions, we narrowed down our search to nine candidate target proteins potentially modulated by XTS (Fig. 6C). Strikingly, the majority of these proteins are enzymes linked to metabolic processes, hinting that metabolic regulation could be the pivotal mechanism by which XTS exerts its ferroptosis-inhibiting effects. Further exploration into these metabolic enzymes unveiled promising binding interactions between XTS and several key enzymes: aldehyde dehydrogenase (ALDH7A1), phosphoglycerate dehydrogenase (PHGDH), glycogenolytic enzyme (GAA), Xaa-Pro aminopeptidase 1 (XPNPEP1), and ephrin type-B receptor 3 (EPHB3), as demonstrated through rigorous molecular docking simulations (Fig. 6D–I). Complementary CETSA analysis reinforced these findings, showing that XTS binding markedly elevated the thermal stability of these enzymes (Fig. 6J). Collectively, these results imply that XTS exerts its potent anti-ferroptosis effects by stabilizing pivotal metabolic enzymes and thwarting their degradation.

Metabolomics reveals that XTS mitigates post-I/R lipid peroxidation by upregulating l-arginine and GSH
To explore the metabolic changes in cardiac tissue after XTS treatment, we conducted an untargeted metabolomics analysis on heart tissues collected 7 days following I/R surgery from mice. Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed a notable distinction between the metabolic profiles of the XTS-treated and DMSO control groups, suggesting that XTS treatment elicited comprehensive metabolic reprogramming within the cardiac tissue (Fig. 7A). Furthermore, hierarchical clustering heatmap analysis revealed distinct patterns of metabolite expression across the experimental cohorts (Fig. 7B). Quantitative analysis using a volcano plot pinpointed 74 metabolites with significant alterations, encompassing 48 upregulated and 26 downregulated metabolites in the I/R-XTS group compared with the I/R-DMSO group (Fig. 7C). Metabolic pathway analysis revealed that XTS disrupted purine metabolism, notably leading to an upregulation of uric acid—the final breakdown product of purine catabolism (Fig. 7D–E). Strikingly, XTS treatment resulted in elevated myocardial L-arginine levels (Fig. 7F). Mechanistically, l-arginine acts as a substrate for nitric oxide synthase (NOS), catalyzing the production of NO in the presence of oxygen and nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH). This process helps mitigate excessive ROS generation and alleviate I/R injury [28, 29]. Additionally, XTS treatment was associated with increased levels of GSH, a crucial cofactor for GPX4. This observation implies that XTS may enhance resistance to ferroptosis by maintaining GPX4 enzymatic activity through a GSH-dependent mechanism.

RNA sequencing reveals XTS-mediated suppression of ferroptosis in cardiomyocytes under H/R injury
To comprehensively explore the transcriptomic impact of XTS intervention on cardiomyocytes, we conducted RNA-seq on H9c2 cell lines from three distinct groups: Control, H/R, and XTS-treated. Our sample distribution analysis unveiled consistent gene expression patterns across all experimental cohorts, attesting to the robustness and reliability of our dataset (Supplementary Fig. 6A). Furthermore, intergroup correlation analysis highlighted notable intragroup consistency, with markedly higher correlation coefficients observed between the Control and XTS groups, contrasting sharply with their significant transcriptional divergence from the H/R group (Fig. 8A). PCA further elucidated distinct clustering patterns in the gene expression landscape, mirroring the unique distribution characteristics of each group (Fig. 8B). However, our differential gene expression analysis revealed a striking contrast: 1535 genes were significantly dysregulated between the Control and H/R groups, while 969 genes exhibited differential expression when comparing XTS with H/R (Fig. 8C; Supplementary 6B, C). Upon cross-comparing these shared differentially expressed genes (DEGs), we observed a notable shift—139 genes were significantly upregulated, whereas 427 were downregulated following XTS treatment (Fig. 8D). Delving deeper, GO enrichment analysis unveiled that the downregulated DEGs after XTS treatment were predominantly involved in crucial pathways such as protein refolding, positive regulation of cell migration, and the hypoxia response (Fig. 8E). Furthermore, KEGG pathway analysis shed light on the marked inhibition of the MAPK and PI3K-Akt signaling pathways post-XTS intervention (Fig. 8F). In contrast, the upregulated DEGs demonstrated a pronounced enrichment in DNA damage repair mechanisms and the mTOR pathway (Fig. 8G, H), highlighting the distinct impacts of XTS treatment on gene regulation and cellular processes.

Discussion
Ischemic heart disease has been identified as a leading cause of mortality worldwide [30]. There is no doubt that emergent revascularization to restore blood flow is the most important treatment for AMI. However, the simultaneously induced ischemia–reperfusion injury commonly resulted in secondary cardiomyocytes death and coronary microvascular damage. Of note, ferroptosis has been confirmed as a critical process in the pathogenesis of cardiac I/R injury [15]. In this study, untargeted metabolomic analyses of heart samples from infarcted neonatal and adult mice initially unveiled a notable enrichment in nucleotide metabolism pathways. Subsequent targeted metabolomic assessments further highlighted a marked accumulation of XTS specifically in the hearts of neonatal mice during the early stages of MI, contrasting sharply with the findings in adult mice. This distinct accumulation pinpointed XTS might be a pivotal factor in the repair process following MI. On the basis of the endogenous cardioprotective phenotype of XTS in neonatal mice, we employed a preconditioning administration strategy to elevate XTS concentrations in adult myocardial tissue and primary cardiomyocytes. Both in vivo and in vitro experiments demonstrated that XTS treatment substantially mitigated cardiac remodeling and enhanced cardiac function after I/R injury. This beneficial effect might be primarily attributed to the significant reduction of hypoxia-induced ferroptosis.
During the perinatal period, cardiomyocytes can exhibit dramatic fluctuations in DNA synthesis rates [31], suggesting significant changes in nucleotide metabolism. The metabolic flux of nucleotides is tightly regulated by the balance between de novo synthesis, salvage pathways, and degradation pathways of purine and pyrimidine nucleotides. Beyond serving as fundamental building blocks of DNA and RNA, purine metabolites can provide essential energy and cofactors to support cellular survival and proliferation [32]. Previous studies had demonstrated that the purine degradation pathway would be upregulated in the postnatal heart, and blocking purine degradation via inhibition of xanthine oxidase (XO) could significantly reduce the production of ROS [12]. It has been well established that ischemic injury can significantly reduce oxygen and glucose supply, leading to abnormal glucose metabolism, including disruptions in the glycolytic pathway and the tricarboxylic acid cycle (TCA) [33]. On the other hand, it is well known that ATP will be degraded to AMP, which is subsequently broken down into adenosine, followed by inosine, hypoxanthine, and xanthine, eventually forming uric acid to be excreted [34]. As a complement to existing research, our untargeted metabolomics analysis uncovered that purine metabolites in both neonatal and adult mice following MI grouped into three distinct modules. Notably, the purine degradation pathway was markedly activated in both age groups post-MI, manifested by the considerable accumulation of end-products such as xanthine and uric acid. A striking observation was that neonatal mice had a lower baseline activity in the purine degradation pathway, potentially playing a pivotal role in their diminished production of ROS and heightened regenerative capacity after MI. This reduced baseline purine degradation in neonatal mice might therefore be a crucial determinant of their superior resilience and repair mechanisms compared with adults.
Previous studies had shown that intermediates of the purine metabolism pathway, such as adenosine and inosine, exerted protective effects against ischemic injury through adenosine receptors [35–37]. Our study further indicated that XTS could be progressively degraded during cardiac development, but would be significantly accumulated after MI in neonatal hearts. However, XTS treatment could not promote cardiomyocyte proliferation or hypertrophy after I/R injury in adult mice, the observed cardioprotective effects might result from the significant reduction of lipid peroxidation products, as well as its relevant processes. XTS had be identified as an intermediate metabolite in the purine metabolism pathway, often synthesized from XMP under the catalytic action of NT5C2 and then would be degraded into xanthine following the activation of PNP. On the other hand, XTS used to be reported to regulate hepatic glucose homeostasis through inhibiting gluconeogenesis while activating glycogenesis [38]. Besides, XTS could also serve as a precursor for caffeine biosynthesis [39]. In Arabidopsis, XTS could act as the primary intermediate in purine nucleotide degradation and was hydrolyzed into xanthine and ribose by a complex composed of two nucleoside hydrolases [40]. Additionally, XTS was also found to be positively correlated with the favorable biomarker of kidney injury in obese patients, such as urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR), suggesting its potential protective effects [41]. Earlier detection of XTS-modified RNA unveiled that XTS has the ability to forge unique base pairing configurations with diverse nucleotides, thereby modulating its recognition by reverse transcriptase and shedding light on its probable biological importance [42].
Ferroptosis, a newly identified regulated form of cell death, primarily involves three pathways: the classical glutathione-GPX4 ferroptosis regulatory axis, the ferroptosis suppressor protein 1 (FSP1)-coenzyme Q10 system, and lipid peroxidation suppression mediated by squalene and biopterin (BH2/BH4) [43]. Ferroptosis is a major cause of cardiomyocyte loss post AMI, which can be observed in both neonatal and adult mouse hearts [15, 20]. Emerging evidence indicates that ferroptosis is implicated in I/R and metabolism disorder [44]. Although we did not investigate changes in cytosolic ROS-producing XO activity after XTS treatment, our study found that XTS treatment could significantly reduce mitochondrial-derived ROS through upregulating GPX4 expressions on the basis of both in vivo and in vitro experiments. Possible explanations might be associated with the alterations in purine nucleotide levels triggered by myocardial infarction in neonatal mice could signify a biological defense mechanism against myocardial ischemia.
Our study revealed that XTS effectively inhibited the degradation of various metabolic enzymes within cardiomyocytes, enzymes that play pivotal roles in regulating lipid peroxidation and ferroptosis. Commonly, ALDH7A1 employs a dual-pronged mechanism to suppress ferroptosis: first, its enzymatic activity directly mitigates oxidative damage by eliminating reactive aldehyde byproducts of lipid peroxidation; secondly, it generates membrane-associated NADH, enhancing the antioxidant capabilities of FSP1. Furthermore, the membrane localization of ALDH7A1, facilitated by 5′-Adenosine monophosphate-activated protein kinase (AMPK), promotes the recruitment and stabilization of FSP1, collectively forming a robust defensive network [45]. Additionally, PHGDH can interact with PCBP2 to upregulate SLC7A11 expression, thereby inhibiting ferroptosis and driving the progression of bladder cancer [46]. Most importantly, the protective effects of XTS against ferroptosis are likely attributed to its ability to shield these crucial enzymes from proteasomal degradation, thereby ensuring their continued functionality.
This study has several limitations that warrant discussion. First, the precise upstream sources of xanthine derivative accumulation observed during the cardiac regeneration phase post-myocardial infarction remain undefined. Further investigations employing dynamic 13C nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy could elucidate the intricate mechanisms of purine metabolism in contracting cardiomyocytes, thereby establishing causal links between metabolic shifts and pathological outcomes following I/R injury. Second, while we identified and validated multiple XTS-binding proteins associated with oxidative stress resistance, this study did not experimentally determine which specific protein(s) mediate XTS’s protective effects against ferroptosis in cardiomyocytes. Finally, the clinical implications of XTS-associated metabolic alterations—particularly whether sustained elevation of serum uric acid secondary to XTS administration could adversely affect long-term cardiac function—require rigorous evaluation through extended longitudinal studies. In addition, although our study highlights the cardioprotective benefits of XTS preconditioning, it is important to recognize that we did not conduct comparisons with interventions applied during or after injury. To further substantiate the advantages of XTS, additional comparisons with other cardioprotective strategies are still necessary.
Conclusions
XTS, a key intermediate in purine metabolism, enhances cardiac remodeling and function following I/R injury by suppressing hypoxia-induced ferroptosis and subsequently reducing mitochondrial ROS production. These findings offer novel insights into the potential therapeutic benefits of XTS as an adjunctive treatment for patients with AMI undergoing emergency revascularization.
