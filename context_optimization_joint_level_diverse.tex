\subsection{Context Optimization}
\label{sec:context-optimization}

The evidence corpus supplies a large number of relevant observations, but the context length of the reasoning model is limited. We therefore formulate evidence selection as a submodular optimization problem, selecting evidence that represents the best landscape around the novel molecule $q$ for prediction, integrating the property trnasfer model as well as other key deimsnions.

Specifically, we define key three axes central to our optimization: \textbf{smilarity, informativeness, and diversity}. We want to reprsent evidence that is likely to transfer to our actualy nove molecule, otherwise the evndiecen is not relevant. We wantt to represent evidence that is also actaully inforamtive of the prediction task at hand. Further, we want to represent a diverse set of envidence that the language model can collectively reason about as increasingly retrieving informatoin in a narrow subset of eivnidce may display diminishing returns.

Given scores that represent these dimensions, we select evidence from separate direct and indirect candidate pools, $\mathcal{C}_D$ and $\mathcal{C}_I$, with record budgets $B_D$ and $B_I$. For a selected set $S$, let $S_D=S\cap\mathcal{C}_D$ and $S_I=S\cap\mathcal{C}_I$. We formulate selection as
\begin{equation}
    S^\star
    \in
    \operatorname*{arg\,max}_{\substack{
        S\subseteq\mathcal{C}_D\cup\mathcal{C}_I\\
        |S_D|=B_D,\;|S_I|=B_I
    }}
    F(S;q,t),
    \label{eq:context-selection}
\end{equation}
Let $B=B_D+B_I=|S|$ denote the total record budget. The objective combines scores for learned transfer liklihood, molecular similarity, semantic infomartiveness, and molecular, semantic, and evidence-level diversity:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    F(S;q,t)
    ={}&\underbrace{\frac{1}{B}\sum_{i\in S}G_i}_{\text{Similarity}}
    +\underbrace{\lambda_{\mathrm{sr}}\frac{1}{B}
        \sum_{i\in S}w_i}_{\text{Informativeness}}\\
    &+\underbrace{\lambda_{\mathrm{mol}}D_{\mathrm{mol}}(S)
        +\lambda_{\mathrm{sd}}D_{\mathrm{sem}}(S)
        +\lambda_{\mathrm{ld}}D_{\mathrm{level}}(S_I)}_{\text{Diversity}}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:context-objective}
\end{equation}

All weights are nonnegative.

\textbf{Similarity} Here, $s_i=s(m_i,q)$ is the Morgan fingerprint Tanimoto similarity between the source molecule $m_i$ of record $i$ and the query $q$. For records with an assay-transfer score $a_i$, the normalized gated transfer is
\begin{equation}
    G_i=\frac{s_i+\lambda_{\mathrm{ga}}s_i a_i}
    {1+\lambda_{\mathrm{ga}}}.
    \label{eq:gated-relevance}
\end{equation}

While the property transfer tool is better than morganfingerpint in retreiving a more simialr neighborhood to $q$ (See Table X), we notice that the LLM recognizes structures, motifs, and functional groups in the molecules with higher structural diversity allows it reason about transferrability from structural arguments, and this is key for the language model's performance. Thus, we reward our transfer score to be relevant for more structurally similar molecules.

\textbf{Informativeness} To estimate informativeness, how informative a endpoint $p_r$ is for predicting the target property $t$, motivated to exact the kowledge prior inLLMs, we ask an LLM to rank and score endpoints by their usefulness for predicting $t$. Due to the number endpoints, we apply tree-like search procedure to combine the endpoints into more unified smeanti groups.

To generate the weightss, we use two linear passes over the data to achieve this. First, we batch process 12 semantic groups at a time and assign weights to each group. Then, a second pass sorts the semantic group, and recalibrates the weights in a sliding window fashion in which we feed the initiail or previous set of weights as a seed for the next set of weights. Akin to convolution, multiple passes of sliding windows ensures a more global aclibrtion of the weights without exploding the LLM's context window. Furthermore, to ensure consistency and speed up rollout (avoiding long tails), we run each scoring 16 times and grab the first 4 weihgts and average them. The resulting semantci group scores $w_i$ remain fixed during evidence selection.

\textbf{Diversity} The molecular diversity term rewards coverage of Morgan fingerprint features:
\begin{equation}
    D_{\mathrm{mol}}(S)
    =\frac{C_{\mathrm{mol}}(S)}{C_{\mathrm{mol}}^{\mathrm{greedy}}(B)},
    \qquad
    C_{\mathrm{mol}}(S)
    =\frac{\left|\bigcup_{i\in S}\mathcal{B}_i\right|}
    {\left|\bigcup_{j\in\mathcal{C}_D\cup\mathcal{C}_I}\mathcal{B}_j\right|},
    \label{eq:molecular-diversity}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{B}_i$ is the set of active Morgan fingerprint bits for record $i$, and $C_{\mathrm{mol}}^{\mathrm{greedy}}(B)$ is the coverage obtained by a deterministic coverage-only greedy selection of $B$ records from the same candidate universe.

Semantic diversity is the number of represented semantic groups, normalized by the record budget:
\begin{equation}
    D_{\mathrm{sem}}(S)
    =\frac{1}{B}\sum_{g\in\mathcal{G}}
    \min\{1,n_g(S)\},
    \label{eq:semantic-diversity}
\end{equation}
where $n_g(S)$ counts selected records in semantic group $g$.

Level diversity encourages balance across the available later evidence levels without imposing a quota or minimum:
\begin{equation}
    D_{\mathrm{level}}(S_I)
    =\frac{1}{Z_{B_I,|\mathcal{L}|}}
    \sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{L}}H_{n_\ell(S_I)},
    \qquad
    H_n=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{1}{j},
    \quad H_0=0,
    \label{eq:level-diversity}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{L}$ is the set of available later levels, $n_\ell(S_I)$ is the number of selected indirect records from level $\ell$, and $Z_{B_I,|\mathcal{L}|}$ is the maximum value of the numerator over all allocations of the $B_I$ indirect records across those levels. Thus, $D_{\mathrm{level}}(S_I)\in[0,1]$, and the diminishing marginal gain $1/(n_\ell+1)$ favors underrepresented levels while permitting the data to determine the final level counts.

With fixed nonnegative evidence scores, the individual-score terms are modular and the diversity terms are monotone submodular. Their nonnegative weighted sum is therefore monotone submodular. We approximately maximize this objective using lazy greedy selection: starting from an empty set, we repeatedly add the record with the largest marginal gain until $B$ records have been selected.

\subsection{Data Cleaning and Pair Construction}
\label{sec:data-cleaning-pair-construction}

Before generating transfer pairs, we construct a canonical record for each surviving physical source observation. We preserve source provenance, canonicalize each molecule to a normalized parent identity, resolve the measurement and unit, classify the measurement as continuous, binary, or ordinal, and assign a source-aware pair-bucket key. The pair-bucket identity uses only intrinsic scientific dimensions---the source, canonical endpoint, canonical unit, and source-specific assay dimensions---and excludes Gold labels, split membership, voter identity, and other benchmark metadata. Exact duplicate records are collapsed only with explicit lineage; protected Gold physical voters and reviewed level assignments survive this step.

\textbf{Eligibility before pairing.} Records are eligible for transfer only when their scientific representation is complete. Continuous records must have a resolved endpoint and structure, a finite scalar, an absolute or endpoint-defined-ratio reference scope, and a resolved canonical unit. Binary and ordinal records must have a declared measurement scale, category identifier, and category rank. Rows that fail these checks are retained in the evidence library but marked ineligible for assay-transfer calibration and pairing. This keeps the retrieval corpus broader than the calibration corpus without allowing unresolved or relative-only measurements to define transfer labels.

\textbf{Stage 3 and pruning.} Stage 3 is first materialized without record pruning. It assigns every record to a pair bucket, marks whether the record is eligible for assay-transfer calibration, and computes bucket-level support together with a per-bucket standard deviation and empirical value/category geometry. A bucket is not usable for calibration when it lacks sufficient deduplicated records or distinct molecules, but this bucket-level eligibility is separate from pruning: pruning never rejects or deletes a whole bucket, and a pruned record remains in the canonical and retrieval libraries with a record-level ineligibility reason. The later V27 construction applies an additional within-parent-to-between-parent variance gate when defining training and OOD buckets.

The pruning pass is applied only after the unpruned Stage 3 artifact is hash-validated. Its candidate set contains eligible continuous records with finite canonical values, valid molecule identities, and a pair-bucket key. Candidate buckets must contain at least 20 finite records and 16 distinct parent molecules. Within each supported bucket, we evaluate the value distribution on a unit-aware geometry. We flag leave-one-out five-standard-deviation tails and two-decade gaps; for BBB we additionally inspect held-out outer-quartile tails. Verified log-scale axes can bypass the two-decade trigger when the raw values fail a normality test and the log transform is explicitly verified. The LLM reviews only flagged target rows, with a small value-spread context from the same bucket, and returns a structured keep/prune decision. Pruning reasons are restricted to a wrong endpoint or quantity, wrong unit or scale, corrupt extraction, or scientifically implausible/out-of-domain evidence; valid extremes and insufficient-evidence cases are retained. Reviewed task-specific manual exclusions are applied through the same fail-closed record-level map.

After review, the library is rebuilt from the same canonical Stage 2 input with the hash-pinned pruning manifest. The final Stage 3 manifest records the canonical-record, auxiliary-mapping, and pruning hashes and validates exact row coverage. Thus, the output preserves the evidence corpus while preventing only explicitly pruned records from contributing to assay-transfer calibration or pair generation.

\textbf{Cleaning before pairing in V10.3 and V27.} V10.3 uses frozen direct Gold context pairs and record-level V26/V27 evidence rather than regenerating pairs from unprocessed paper rows. It restricts indirect candidates to the intended continuous sources and levels, enforces unique pair identifiers, and uses deterministic source- and bucket-aware selection. For Skin Reaction, the final training pool is additionally grouped by the fully rendered prompt: groups with conflicting completions are removed, while same-completion duplicates retain only the smallest pair identifier. Measurement and unit display always uses one complete atomic pair, with a declared source-first or canonical-first policy and no mixing of fields from different origins.

V27 validates that its V10 Stage 3 and level-mapping inputs are current, assigns train/validation/test-gate membership from Gold parent identities, and removes binary rows that would present the same rendered query within a bucket with opposite labels. It applies a second final-query conflict filter to binary and ordinal records, then calibrates buckets using support requirements, the configured within-to-between variance ratio gate, and the V27 OOD size range. Only after these filters are applied are training pairs sampled. A training pair remains within the same level and pair bucket, uses different normalized parent molecules, and obeys record-role and parent-role degree caps; Morgan similarity and transfer-label balance determine the deterministic sampling order. V27 renders reviewed parent SMILES and complete canonical measurement/unit pairs, so pairing and prompting do not reintroduce salts, unresolved units, conflicting categorical labels, or cross-condition comparisons.
